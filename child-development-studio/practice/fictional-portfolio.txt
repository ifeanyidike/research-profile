Fictional learning portfolio — caregiver and professional preparation
=====================================================================
Original adult-education material. Use invented cases and adult role-play only.
No child is assessed, observed, recruited or treated. Do not enter real names,
schools, histories, images, voices, reports or lightly disguised clinical cases.
A completed portfolio is educational evidence, not a qualification or treatment plan.

Caregiver cases — choose one, retaining its age and supplied facts
----------------------------------------------------------------
A: Age four, enjoys a picture book about trains. Points to a familiar picture,
   says "train go", and turns the page. Adult asks repeated colour questions;
   child closes the book. No hearing/developmental history or diagnosis supplied.
   Question: what do we know about interest, communication and partner demands,
   and what should we ask instead of deciding why the child closed the book?
B: Age nine, wants to join a model-building club. Uses speech and an established
   picture board. Arrival is loud and instructions contain several steps. Says
   "too fast" and points toward the board. No assessed treatment needs supplied.
   Question: what communication and environmental access questions belong in a
   caregiver-team discussion? Do not infer a sensory or language diagnosis.
C: Age fifteen, wants to prepare for music group with more control. Prefers a
   private three-item phone checklist to public reminders. No safety assessment,
   diagnosis or complete health history supplied.
   Question: what chosen help, privacy, local transition and unanswered safety
   questions belong in supported planning? Do not remove all assistance by age.

G12 caregiver portfolio — eight sections
-----------------------------------------
1. Priority: the fictional child's stated goal and preferred communication mode.
2. Strengths: supplied examples; distinguish them from assumptions.
3. Observations: source/context, exact action and adult/environment contribution.
4. Unknowns: at least three competing questions; include hearing/health if relevant.
5. Access: ordinary options to discuss, refusal/stop, communication availability.
6. Team questions: five questions with an appropriate role to answer each.
7. Boundaries: assent, privacy, safety/referral and things you cannot conclude/do.
8. Review: a feasible discussion of usefulness, comfort, access and family burden.

Include three authoritative resources with exact titles, age/scope and which
question each helps answer. Do not use an infant source as an adolescent protocol.
Write one adult role-play with an opening, accessible choice, response to refusal
and closing question. Ask a consenting adult partner to challenge an unsupported
inference, an access assumption and a role boundary. Record a concrete revision.
If working alone, write the three strongest counterarguments and label them as
self-review; partner feedback is a separate optional next step, not invented evidence.

Professional preparation case for P16
--------------------------------------
Age sixteen, wants to contribute original ideas in a media club. Uses speech and
typing; prefers private planning support. Reports fatigue and discomfort in a
crowded setting. No diagnosis, clinical examination, assessment scores or complete
history supplied. Do not invent findings to justify a particular discipline.

P16 portfolio — four pages
---------------------------
1. Facts, source attribution, priorities, strengths and explicit unknowns.
2. Discipline-specific questions, alternative explanations, scope and referral.
3. Evidence table: eight authoritative sources; population/age, method, outcome,
   comparison, uncertainty, relevant finding and what it does NOT establish here.
4. Further-training plan: scientific prerequisites, real programme/regulator
   requirements to verify, supervised competencies and honest current limits.

Research proposal — plan only, no study execution
-------------------------------------------------
Question; why it matters to participation; relevant prior work; falsifiable claim
or qualitative research question; justified methods/outcomes; alternatives and
bias; access/cultural issues; ethics/safeguarding/consent/assent; child-data needs;
resources and stopping criteria; completed evidence versus proposed future work.
A supervisor/ethics process must assess any future study involving people.
Use partner review or clearly labelled self-review; neither confers clinical or
ethics approval. Do not recruit, assess, collect recordings or trial an intervention.
